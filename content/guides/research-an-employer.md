A company name or a positive review does not tell you what a particular team does each day. Research is more useful when it produces a few clear questions than when it produces a long list of impressions.

Start with the role you are considering. This guide is a research method, not an employer ranking, background check, or prediction of your experience.

## Match the Employer and the Role

Find the employer's current careers page and the posting you intend to use. Compare the name, role reference, team, and location where supplied. Similar names or a shared brand do not settle whether two pages describe the same organization or role.

The National Careers Service recommends reading the job description and researching the employer's website before an interview. [Interview tips](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice). You can use that preparation to identify questions before applying, too.

If you cannot confirm an unexpected contact or application destination, use [Check Job Offers and Recruiters](/guides/check-job-offers-and-recruiters/) first. An employer profile on a job site does not by itself verify the person who contacted you.

## Give Each Source a Clear Job

The University of Michigan Career Center lists employer websites, company reviews, and industry information among research starting points. [Interviewing Resources](https://careercenter.umich.edu/content/interviewing-resources). Listing those channels does not make every statement within them equally current or independently verified.

Try these distinctions in your notes:

- **Employer statement:** a careers page describes the company as collaborative. This records how the employer describes itself, not proof of how every team works.
- **Role-specific information:** the current posting names a team and responsibilities. Keep its wording separate from general company descriptions.
- **Reported experience:** a review describes one person's experience. Note its date, role, and location if visible; leave missing context unknown.
- **Open question:** the sources do not explain who reviews work or how priorities are set. Turn that gap into a question rather than a conclusion.

For a news item, distinguish the publisher's reporting from a company announcement it quotes. Record the date and scope. Do not treat several pages repeating one announcement as several independent confirmations.

## A Fictional Example: Different Pages, Different Scope

All three details below are invented, not claims about a real employer:

1. A company overview says its teams are distributed across several locations.
2. A current support-role posting lists an office location but does not specify attendance frequency.
3. An older review from a different team describes working from home.

**What the notes support:** the overview describes multiple locations; the posting names an office; the review reports another person's earlier arrangement.

**What remains unknown:** whether this particular role permits remote work and what its team expects now. These pages do not necessarily contradict one another, but they do not answer that question.

**A useful question:** "For this role and team, what office attendance and collaboration hours should I understand?"

This approach avoids treating a general description as a role-specific promise. See [Remote Workers](/career-guides/remote-workers/) for more questions about arrangements.

## Keep a Small Evidence Note

In your own private document, use one entry per point:

- Source URL and page title:
- Publisher or speaker, as shown:
- Publication/update date, if visible, and date you checked:
- Exact role, team, or location the information covers:
- What it actually states:
- What is still unknown:
- One question or next check:

If a page has no date, record that instead of assuming it is recent. Keep summaries short and factual. Do not collect private employee details or try to identify anonymous reviewers.

The [Glassdoor profile](/job-sites/sites/glassdoor/) offers a review-reading starting point; [LinkedIn](/job-sites/sites/linkedin/) and the [job-site comparison](/job-sites/) cover other research channels. A displayed score is not a substitute for understanding the specific role.

## Turn Broad Claims Into Specific Questions

Choose questions the posting has not already answered. These are editorial examples, not a required interview script:

- **"Collaborative team":** which teams would this role work with on a typical task?
- **"Learning opportunities":** what does onboarding for this role involve, and who provides feedback?
- **"Ownership":** which decisions would the person make, and which would need review?
- **"Changing priorities":** how does the team decide what to pause when a new task becomes urgent?

A useful answer adds context; one answer does not establish a complete picture of the workplace. Record what was actually said and keep any remaining uncertainty visible.

## Choose a Next Step Without Scoring the Company

Finish with a short note: what the role appears to involve, what you can substantiate, and what you still need to ask. Do not convert missing information into a positive or negative score.

Then [read the posting more closely](/guides/read-a-job-posting/) or [prepare relevant interview questions](/guides/prepare-for-an-interview/). Revisit time-sensitive information when you next use it, rather than relying indefinitely on an old note.
