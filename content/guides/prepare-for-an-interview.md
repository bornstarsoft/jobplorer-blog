Good preparation starts with the work being discussed, not a script to memorize. Choose a few relevant examples and a few questions whose answers will help you understand the role.

These are optional preparation prompts, not an employer's scoring rubric or a promise of an interview outcome. Keep your notes privately; there is nothing to upload here. If an invitation is unexpected, [check the employer and recruiter first](/guides/check-job-offers-and-recruiters/).

## Start With the Posting

The National Careers Service recommends reviewing the role, employer website, submitted application, and examples from your experience before an interview. Follow the actual invitation's format and instructions. [National Careers Service: Interview tips](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice).

Try this Jobplorer preparation exercise for two or three responsibilities:

- **Posting says:** note a specific responsibility, rather than just the job title.
- **My evidence:** identify something you actually did and can explain.
- **Still unclear:** turn an assumption into a question for the employer.

For example, "maintain internal tools" could lead to a question about who reports problems and how the team prioritizes fixes. Do not infer the whole working process from one phrase.

## Build an Example With STAR

STAR is one way to organize an answer: situation, task, action, and result. The National Careers Service also suggests being ready for follow-up questions. It is a structure to try, not a required format for every question. [National Careers Service: The STAR method](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method).

Here is an invented project example, not a claim to copy into your experience:

- **Situation:** a student project had an intermittent checkout error.
- **Task:** I was responsible for reproducing the issue.
- **Action:** I documented the steps and compared successful and failed attempts, then shared a minimal example with the teammate changing the code.
- **Result:** the team reproduced the problem and tested a fix. I learned to record conditions, not just screenshots.

In your own example, distinguish your contribution from the team's. Describe only results you can support; do not invent a percentage improvement to make an answer sound stronger.

## Questions That Make the Role Clearer

Pick questions that the posting and conversation have not already answered. These are Jobplorer's editorial examples:

- What kind of work would the person in this role start with?
- How does the team review work and give feedback?
- Which responsibilities are shared with other teams?
- What is an example of a problem the team is currently trying to solve?
- For a remote role, which collaboration hours or office visits should I clarify?

A question is useful when its answer changes your understanding, not because it appears on a list. The [Remote Workers guide](/career-guides/remote-workers/) offers more work-arrangement research prompts.

## Check the Video Setup

The National Careers Service distinguishes live conversations from recorded interviews and recommends testing equipment and following the supplied instructions. For a recorded assessment, check the practice area or guidance for time limits and retry options rather than assuming they exist. [National Careers Service: Video interview tips](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/how-to-do-well-in-video-interviews).

Before the session, confirm the time zone, meeting method, and contact for technical problems. Test the microphone, camera, and connection. Close private windows and notifications before any planned screen sharing. Do not expose account details or confidential work to demonstrate a skill.

## After the Conversation

In your private notes, separate what the employer actually said from your interpretation. Record any stated next step, one question that remains open, and one example you would explain more clearly next time. Follow the employer's communication instructions rather than assuming a universal follow-up schedule.
