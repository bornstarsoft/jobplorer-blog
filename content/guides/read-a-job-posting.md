Two roles with the same title can leave you with very different questions. Before adapting an application, work from the actual posting rather than the title alone.

The goal is a clear research note, not a match score or a prediction of who will be selected. Use the employer's current instructions and leave missing information unresolved.

## Start With the Source

Find the current posting on the employer's careers page or its stated application destination. Note the role reference, location, date checked, closing information if supplied, and documents requested. If you cannot confirm an unexpected message, use [Check Job Offers and Recruiters](/guides/check-job-offers-and-recruiters/) first.

The National Careers Service suggests using job descriptions to identify relevant skills and reflecting on experience when identifying your own. Its guidance is a research starting point, not a substitute for an individual employer's criteria. [Understand and develop your skills](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/identifying-skills-and-upskilling).

## Separate Four Kinds of Information

Try these four headings in your own private notes. They are Jobplorer's suggested reading method, not categories every employer uses.

1. **Responsibilities:** what work would the person do? Preserve the verb and its object, such as "document support procedures," instead of reducing everything to a tool name.
2. **Stated requirements:** keep the employer's wording and labels. Do not quietly turn an explicit requirement into a preference.
3. **Preferences:** record items described as preferred, desirable, or helpful separately. Do not turn them into universal requirements either.
4. **Open questions:** identify information the posting does not settle, such as the team's workflow or how often a named tool is used.

If a condition is unclear, seek clarification through the verified employer contact. This guide does not determine eligibility or tell you to disregard a requirement.

## A Fictional Posting, Read Carefully

Consider this invented excerpt: "Maintain support articles, investigate recurring product issues, and work with engineers. Familiarity with SQL is preferred. Hybrid arrangement; details discussed during the process."

- **What it states:** the work includes documentation, investigation, and collaboration. SQL appears as a preference in this example.
- **Possible evidence to describe:** a time you clarified confusing instructions or documented how to reproduce a problem, if you actually did that work.
- **What it does not establish:** office frequency, who prioritizes issues, or which tools the team uses day to day. Those remain questions.

Nothing in the excerpt establishes that a candidate is qualified, that training will be provided, or that the arrangement fits a particular location. Do not fill those gaps with an optimistic assumption.

## Connect a Responsibility to Evidence

The National Careers Service's application guidance recommends relevant experience and specific examples when asked about skills. It includes examples beyond paid employment, such as volunteering and activities. [Application form advice](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/application-forms).

For each responsibility you choose to discuss, try a three-line note:

- **The posting asks for:** one specific activity.
- **I can explain:** a truthful example, your contribution, and the evidence available.
- **I need to clarify:** one remaining question, without inventing experience to fill the gap.

If you have no supporting example, record that honestly. A keyword in the posting is not a reason to add a skill you cannot explain. For a more detailed work example, use [Build a Project Case Study](/guides/build-a-project-case-study/).

## Compare Two Roles Without Scoring Yourself

Use the same four headings for each posting. Compare the work described and the questions left open, not just titles or repeated keywords. A role with fewer unanswered questions is not automatically the better role; it simply gives you different information to work with.

Finish with one next action: verify an unclear detail, prepare a relevant example, or review the application instructions. The [resume checklist](/posts/why-87-percent-of-job-seekers-abandon-their-resumes/) and [interview preparation guide](/guides/prepare-for-an-interview/) cover those later steps.
