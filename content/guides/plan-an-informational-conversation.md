A posting can name responsibilities without explaining what doing them feels like. A conversation with someone familiar with the work can give you another perspective, provided you keep its scope clear.

This guide is for learning about a role, not requesting a referral or preparing for a selection interview. Jobplorer does not arrange introductions or send messages. There is no contact list or personal information to submit here.

## Define a Question, Not a Job Request

UC Berkeley Career Engagement describes informational interviews as career-exploration conversations rather than job interviews or requests for openings. [Informational Interviews](https://www.career.berkeley.edu/start-exploring/informational-interviews/).

Choose one thing you want to understand before choosing whom to approach. For example: "How does someone in a technical support role decide whether instructions are clear enough?" That is a narrower starting point than "Tell me everything about your career."

Read public information first. Use [Read a Job Posting](/guides/read-a-job-posting/) and [Research an Employer Before Applying](/guides/research-an-employer/) to separate facts you already have from questions still open. If you have an actual interview invitation, use [Prepare for an Interview](/guides/prepare-for-an-interview/) instead.

## Choose an Appropriate Contact

The National Careers Service includes people known through work or study and professional online communities among networking channels. It recommends starting with a clear, modest request. [Networking: tips to help your career](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/networking).

Consider a professional channel where career questions are welcome. A public profile is not permission to find someone's private phone number or send repeated messages. Follow the community's rules and the person's stated preferences. Alumni-only services may require membership; do not assume they are open to everyone.

Explain honestly how you found the person. Do not invent a mutual contact, claim to have read work you have not read, or imply that Jobplorer introduced you. For an unexpected approach to you, use [Check Job Offers and Recruiters](/guides/check-job-offers-and-recruiters/) before sharing documents.

## Make a Small, Honest Request

This is an invented request, not a message to a real person. Replace the bracketed placeholders with truthful details and change the role or topic to match your research. The suggested 15 minutes is only an example, not a required meeting length.

> Hello [name], I found your [public article or talk you actually read] while researching technical support work. I would like to understand how support teams check that setup instructions are clear. If you are open to a short online conversation, perhaps 15 minutes, I would appreciate hearing about your experience. I am looking for perspective on the work, not a referral. I understand if you are unavailable. Thank you, [your name].

Keep the reason specific and give the person room to decline. Do not attach identity documents, an application bundle, or confidential work samples to this kind of initial request.

If they agree, confirm the topic, time zone, channel, and available time. Do not add an automated recording or transcription service. For this exercise, use a few brief private learning notes instead of recording the conversation.

## Ask About Work, Not Confidential Details

These are Jobplorer's optional questions, not an employer assessment or a script you need to complete:

- What is an ordinary task in the part of the role you know?
- How do you check whether that task is complete or useful?
- What did you misunderstand about this work before doing it?
- Which other roles do you collaborate with on that task?
- Is there a public resource that would help me explore this further?

Let the person skip any question. Do not ask for customer records, private employee information, unreleased plans, or internal assessment answers. A general description is enough; you are not collecting proof of someone else's work.

Ask a clarifying question when needed: "Is that how your current team works, or something you experienced in a previous role?" Do not assume the answer describes every organization with the same job title.

## Keep One Account in Context

Here is a fictional conversation note, not a claim about any real workplace:

- **Reported experience:** a support specialist says their previous team tested instructions before publishing updates.
- **What this supports:** one person's account of a process in that earlier team.
- **What remains unknown:** how other teams work, whether a current vacancy includes this task, and which tools it uses.
- **Next check:** revisit the current posting and ask a role-specific question through the employer's stated channel if needed.

In your own notes, record the date, role context shared, useful takeaway, uncertainty, and next check. Avoid unnecessary contact details or verbatim transcripts. Do not publish or attribute the person's comments without their agreement.

A conversation is not a reference, endorsement, or promise of an opportunity. Keep the person's experience separate from your conclusions.

## Close With a Useful Next Step

Finish within the agreed time. A short thank-you can name what helped without introducing an unrequested favor. This second example is also fictional; use it only when its details match what happened:

> Thank you for explaining how your previous team checked support instructions. I will compare that example with the responsibilities in the roles I am researching. The distinction between writing an instruction and checking whether someone can follow it was useful.

Choose one manageable action: read a suggested public resource, refine a question, or try a small practice task. If you create something yourself, the [project case study guide](/guides/build-a-project-case-study/) helps distinguish your work from advice you received. Bring the open question to your [weekly job search review](/job-seeker-tools/#your-weekly-job-search-review).

## If There Is No Reply

No response is not evidence that you are unsuitable for a role or that an employer rejected an application. Do not turn silence into a score or keep contacting the person across different channels.

You can continue through public career resources and employer materials without a private conversation. Start with the [career guides](/career-guides/) or [job-site comparison](/job-sites/). A personal introduction is not required to use these research paths.
