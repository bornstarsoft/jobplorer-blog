A convincing message is a reason to check, not proof that a job or recruiter is genuine. Start with one question: can you confirm this opportunity through a channel you found independently?

This is general research guidance, not an investigation of a particular employer or legal advice. Passing a checklist does not certify an offer as safe.

## Before You Reply

Use this short research sequence before sending application documents:

1. **Find the employer independently.** Open its website through a route you already trust, rather than relying only on the message's link.
2. **Look for the role.** Compare the title, location, reference number, and application destination with the employer's careers page. A missing listing is unresolved information, not proof of fraud or proof that the role exists.
3. **Confirm the contact.** If something does not match, ask through contact details found independently on the employer's website. A staffing agency may be involved; verify that relationship rather than guessing.
4. **Separate facts from promises.** Keep a brief private note of what the official source confirms and what still needs an answer. Do not send Jobplorer identity documents or recruiter correspondence.

This sequence is Jobplorer's editorial research checklist. It cannot authenticate a person or guarantee a safe transaction.

## Warning Signs Worth Pausing For

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission describes job scams that seek money or personal information, including payment to obtain a job and fake checks that ask recipients to forward funds. Its advice also cautions that finding no complaints does not establish that a company is honest. [FTC: Job Scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams).

- **Payment to secure the position:** pause instead of treating an upfront payment as an ordinary application step.
- **A check followed by a spending or refund instruction:** do not treat an apparent deposit as evidence that the offer is genuine.
- **Pressure for sensitive information before questions are answered:** independently verify the contact and the process before proceeding.

The FTC's recruiter alert describes convincing-looking invitations and offers that push for financial or identity details before an interview. An impressive logo or detailed job description is not enough to resolve those concerns. [FTC: Job scammers are looking to hire you](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/07/job-scammers-are-looking-hire-you).

## A Fictional Message to Think Through

Imagine an unexpected message that names a familiar company, promises a remote role, and tells you to purchase equipment from a particular contact before discussing the work.

Do not let the familiar company name answer the separate questions: who sent the message, does the employer recognize the role, and why is a payment being requested? Find the official employer channel yourself. If you cannot resolve the mismatch, leave the opportunity unverified rather than supplying more information to keep the conversation moving.

This example is invented for practice and does not describe a named employer.

## When Something Still Does Not Add Up

Keep the message and posting URL in your own records. Use the platform's official reporting channel if the posting or contact appears suspicious. For U.S. consumer guidance, the FTC's [Job Scams page](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams) links to its reporting and recovery resources. Readers elsewhere should use the relevant official consumer-protection resource for their location. This page does not promise a recovery outcome.

You do not need to decide whether a message is legally fraudulent in order to pause and verify it.
