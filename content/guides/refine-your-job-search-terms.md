If a search keeps returning work you do not mean, start with the words and settings you used. A result list is a starting point for research, not a complete picture of the market or a judgment of your suitability.

This guide helps you plan searches on job boards. It does not run a search, show live vacancies, or create alerts. No resume, contact details, or personal profile is needed to work through the fictional examples.

## Describe the Work Before Choosing a Title

Write one sentence about the activity you want to investigate. For example: "I want to understand roles that help users resolve product setup problems and improve support instructions."

Then separate three things in your own notes:

- **Work:** the tasks you want to explore.
- **Possible search terms:** titles or phrases worth testing, not confirmed equivalents.
- **Details to verify:** responsibilities, location, working arrangement, and the employer's stated criteria.

Indeed's UK help explains that titles can differ by company and suggests trying related terms. It also notes that available filters can depend on the search and location. [Improving your job searches](https://www.indeed.com/help/job-seekers/articles/204488950-improving-your-job-searches-tips-and-help?co=GB&hl=en).

Do not copy a term into your application as a skill just because it appeared in search results. Use [Read a Job Posting](/guides/read-a-job-posting/) to inspect what a specific role actually asks for.

## Make a Small Search Plan

These are three hypothetical trials, not searches that Jobplorer has run or measured. Keep your chosen location and other settings the same while comparing the wording:

1. **`support`:** a broad starting term. Read descriptions to see which kinds of work appear.
2. **`technical support`:** a different phrase to investigate. Check whether the work concerns customer products, internal equipment, or something else.
3. **`product support`:** another candidate phrase. Compare the responsibilities rather than assuming it means the same job as the previous trial.

For each trial, record the exact terms, date, site, settings, and one useful observation. A note such as "this description focuses on internal equipment" is more informative than "bad search."

You can revise the plan after reading a few descriptions. The point is to learn which wording describes the work, not to collect the largest result count.

## Compare Descriptions, Not Similar-Sounding Titles

All three snippets below are invented. They are not live postings, employer endorsements, or a ranking of occupations:

1. **Support Specialist:** responds to product setup questions and updates support articles.
2. **Technical Support Analyst:** monitors internal devices and escalates hardware incidents.
3. **Customer Success Associate:** plans account check-ins and discusses renewals.

For the example goal above, the first description directly mentions setup help and documentation. It is a reason to inspect that posting more closely, not proof that the applicant meets its requirements.

The second and third examples describe different work despite sharing some support-related language. None supplies enough information to decide location, seniority, or suitability. Real postings may combine responsibilities differently; do not treat these invented titles as a fixed classification.

## Change One Setting at a Time

Once the wording is useful, inspect the filters available on the site you are using. A simple comparison is easier to interpret when you know exactly what changed:

- Keep the query and location fixed while trying one optional filter.
- Read the setting's label and current help instead of assuming what it excludes.
- Note the change, then inspect full descriptions rather than just snippets.
- If the results become less useful, undo that one change before trying another.

This is an editorial troubleshooting method, not a controlled experiment on a search algorithm. Listings and result order can change between visits. A different result list does not establish that one setting caused every difference.

Use a location or arrangement you genuinely want to investigate. Do not broaden a search and then assume the displayed roles meet your practical needs. In particular, inspect the location details behind a remote label using the [Remote Workers guide](/career-guides/remote-workers/).

## Check Syntax Before Using Search Operators

Indeed's linked help describes operators and phrase searches for its own service. Do not assume that quotes, a minus sign, `OR`, or `title:` behave the same way on every board, employer site, or country version.

Start with plain terms. Use advanced syntax only after checking the current help for that site and confirming that the returned descriptions reflect your intended search. An unfamiliar operator is not a shortcut to assessing a person's qualifications.

The [job-site comparison](/job-sites/), [Indeed profile](/job-sites/sites/indeed/), and [LinkedIn profile](/job-sites/sites/linkedin/) provide separate starting points for researching the channels themselves.

## Verify a Result Before Saving a Next Action

The National Careers Service lists job boards and employer careers websites as places to look for vacancies. [Where to find job vacancies](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/advertised-job-vacancies).

When a result interests you, check the employer, role reference if supplied, current description, and application destination. Similar titles alone do not establish that two listings are the same vacancy. If a job board and employer page appear to describe the same role but disagree, record the difference and clarify it before relying on either summary.

If a search returns little useful information, check spelling, unfamiliar syntax, and active filters. Treat it as a reason to review the search or another source, not evidence that no suitable work exists anywhere. For an unexpected contact or an unclear application destination, use [Check Job Offers and Recruiters](/guides/check-job-offers-and-recruiters/).

## Keep Alerts and Notes Deliberate

An optional alert on an external site should follow a search you understand. Check that site's current availability, account requirements, notification controls, and data-handling information before enabling anything. Do not assume an alert includes every vacancy or means an application was submitted.

You can instead keep a short note in your own private document: query, site, settings, date checked, observation, and next action. Avoid putting personal documents, account credentials, or private employer information into a search box.

At your [weekly job search review](/job-seeker-tools/#your-weekly-job-search-review), revisit the useful phrases and remove assumptions that did not hold up. Keep the next step concrete: inspect a posting, clarify a detail, or try one different term.
